What I'm brought in to do

Three pivots.
Most programmes are stuck at one of them.

AI programmes rarely fail on technology. They stall at a transition nobody owns. These are the three I'm called in for. They run roughly in order, though most organisations are working on two at once, and the second is where the majority are sitting right now.

PIVOT 01
Pilot → Production
Make it real, in front of customers
The demo worked. Production requires answering what happens when the model is wrong in front of a customer, who approves that, and what the fallback is. Those are decisions, not engineering tasks, which is why they get deferred.
The tell: an eighteen-month-old pilot everyone still calls promising.
→
PIVOT 02
Initial → Industrialised
Make the second one cheaper than the first
One use case in production is an achievement. Twenty is a capability. That needs shared foundations, governed data, and a delivery pattern others can follow, rather than a hero team rebuilding from scratch each time.
The tell: every new use case costs what the first one did.
→
PIVOT 03
Managed → Resilient
Make it survive without me, and without you
Three shifts at once: it holds up when models drift or vendors change underneath it, it can be defended to a regulator with evidence rather than assurances, and your own team runs it confidently after I leave.
The tell: nobody can explain last Tuesday's decision.

How I work

Architecture is a stance,
not a diagram.

"Anyone can wire up an intelligent system now. The hard part is sensibility: knowing which problem deserves the intelligence, what the output means in context, and when to overrule a confident machine. That judgment is the architecture. The diagram is just its residue."

After twenty-five years across regulated industries, the pattern holds. The platforms that lasted were the ones where someone supplied judgment the models could not, the sensibility to frame the right question before engineering the right answer.

01

Frame the right problem first

Sensibility starts before the model. The costliest mistakes are intelligent answers to the wrong question.

02

Engineer context into the system

Semantic layers, governance, lineage. The infrastructure that lets a model know what its output actually means.

03

Then engineer for excellence

Kafka, Lakehouse, RAG, agentic AI. The technology earns its place once the judgment layer is set.

04

Keep a human in the override seat

Design the system so judgment can intervene. Sensibility at machine speed still needs a person who can say "stop."

ForA CTO or AI platform owner whose agent works in the demo and fails in production.

I build a domain test set with your own experts, run each case repeatedly, and report how often the agent is right and how it fails. Then I review the design around it: fixed workflow or agent, retrieval permissions, document preparation, decision record retention, running cost.

You keepA reliability number the board can act on, and a design your team can build.

Regulatory · Major BankPivot 03 · Resilient
The data was fine. The accountability wasn't.
Regulators asked questions the data couldn't answer. The cause: twelve teams, twelve versions of the truth, no shared ownership.
Federated governance on Data Vault 2.0, led by a governance CoE that gave every team ownership rather than obligation. Trust first, platform second.
✓Systemic risk transparency restored; teams aligned
National Tax AuthorityPivot 01 · Production
A capable platform nobody would use.
Investigators ignored automated fraud scores. Nobody had explained the logic, so the spend sat idle as expensive shelfware.
Redesigned the investigator workflow alongside the model. Co-designed the interface and built transparency into every score with the people using it.
10%YoY tax base growth · 50% faster resolution
Real-Time PaymentsPivot 03 · Resilient
The ops team was overriding the system.
A Kafka fraud platform performed well technically, but operations overrode it routinely because the alerts made no sense to them.
Rebuilt the alert logic with the fraud team: thresholds, explanations, exception flows co-designed, then re-engineered the platform around those decisions.
↓Override rate collapsed; capture rate up
GenAI & RAG · LendingPivot 01 · Production
Analysts felt replaced, so they opted out.
An AI-assisted KYC system was rejected by the lending team. Adoption near zero: the AI decided, the analysts watched.
Reframed it as a copilot. Rebuilt the experience so analysts stayed the decision-makers with AI surfacing evidence. Workshops ran before code did.
↑Adoption transformed; processing time down
Predictive AnalyticsPivot 01 · Production
A good model, ignored.
A sound churn model output probability scores. Managers ignored it: a number between 0 and 1 told them nothing actionable.
Replaced scores with plain-language narratives and ranked actions. Rebuilt around the only question that mattered: what do I do next?
→From ignored to cited in every review
ORIAN.Data PlatformPivot 02 · Industrialised
Brilliant outputs, no judgment behind them.
AI investments kept producing confident answers nobody could stand behind. Teams couldn't explain decisions, executives couldn't verify them.
Architected ORIAN.Data so judgment is structural: a Semantic Core that gives outputs context and a Trust pillar that makes them explainable.
★Deployed across regulated-industry clients
Global Lakehouse · AzurePivot 02 · Industrialised
The argument was political, not technical.
A multi-million Lakehouse stalled at six months. Every division defined the same metric differently. Technology was waiting on alignment.
Ran business-alignment workshops before architecture. Built a shared glossary every division signed off, then designed the platform around that language.

Data Mesh · Enterprise BankPivot 02 · Industrialised
The central team was a bottleneck, not a service.
Thirty people served twelve domains. Requests took six to eight weeks. Domains felt powerless; the team felt unappreciated.
Led the cultural shift first: domain leads designed their own data products in workshops. The central team became enablers; the mesh followed the culture.
↑Domain autonomy; faster insight; valued team

11,748 tables, 49.65 TB, 1,550 ETL objects. The plan was solid; the engineers were anxious because they couldn't see the whole picture.
Built full sprint-by-sprint, risk-by-risk visibility. Every engineer could see what moved, when, and what rollback looked like. Transparency replaced fear.
