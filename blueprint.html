A working reference, not a white paper

Seven decisions
between a pilot and a business result.

Most enterprise AI programmes do not fail on the model. They fail on seven decisions that are made badly, made late, or never assigned to anyone. This is what the evidence says about each one as of September 2026, including where the evidence contradicts itself, and who in your organisation has to own the answer.

Reference architecture

How data and knowledge become trusted decisions. Data comes in on the left, is governed and given shared meaning in the middle layers, is reasoned over by the models at the core, and leaves on the right as answers, insights and actions for people and agents. Judgment and trust controls sit around the edge; the control plane runs underneath. Read it as the destination, not the starting point: each use case lights up only the parts it needs, and the gate in decision 02 tells you which part to fund next. Every decision below names where it sits on this picture.

How to read this

Seven considerations, in the order they actually bite. Four of them have to be settled before a pilot starts, because getting them wrong is what makes the pilot unrepeatable. One governs the crossing itself. Two only begin once the system is carrying real traffic.

Before you quote any number in this document

The published evidence on enterprise AI is unusually contaminated, and knowing how tells you which numbers to trust. Four things explain nearly every apparent contradiction in this field:

  • The denominator. "95 percent of pilots fail" and "25 percent of pilots succeed" are the same dataset counted against different populations.
  • The time window. A study demanding sustained P&L impact within six months and one measuring value realisation at thirteen months will disagree violently while both being correct.
  • What counts as a return. "Do you see positive ROI" and "have you established ROI" are different questions asked of the same seniority of executive, and they return 98 percent and 7 percent respectively.
  • Who paid. Nearly every optimistic headline in this field was commissioned by a party selling the thing it measures. So was at least one of the pessimistic ones.

Where I cite a number, I name the source and the date. Where sources disagree, I state both cases before taking a position. Where I could not verify something, I say so rather than rounding it into a claim.

Objectives, ROI and ownership

If nobody can name the person accountable for what this costs and what it returns, the programme has already chosen its outcome.

On the reference architecture
Human / Business JudgmentObservability & EvaluationContinuous Learning & Feedback

Ownership and cost sit around and beneath the rings, because no layer can supply them. Cost is measured in the control plane's observability and evaluation service; the outcome it is divided by is captured in the continuous learning and feedback loop along the bottom; the person accountable for both is the human and business judgment at the edge.

This is the decision most organisations skip, and it is the one with the cleanest evidence behind it. Scaling has moved. Margin has not.

Adoption rose roughly six points year on year while EBIT attribution stayed flat. That gap is the entire problem. More organisations are running AI in more functions and the same proportion can point to a margin outcome. Scale is not converting.

Cost visibility is the variable that moves ROI

The strongest single finding in the 2026 evidence base is not about models or use cases. It is about accounting. Organisations with strong visibility into AI spend are five times more likely to have established ROI, 15 percent against 3 percent (KPMG, June 2026). The mechanism is not mysterious: you cannot establish a return on a cost you cannot see.

Token cost has become a board-level line item faster than most finance functions adapted. In EY's May 2026 wave of 534 senior leaders, 82 percent were concerned about token consumption and cost, and 98 percent of those using token-consuming tools said cost had made them reconsider their approach: 37 percent reassessing functional priorities, 35 percent reconsidering use cases, 32 percent reconsidering who gets access. Only 64 percent actively monitor token usage against a budget. The FinOps Foundation's 2026 survey found that 98 percent of FinOps practitioners now manage AI spend, up from 63 percent in 2025 and 31 percent in 2024, the fastest scope expansion in the survey's history.

Ownership: the numbers on naming one person

KPMG's June 2026 study is the closest thing to a controlled test of single-owner accountability. Where the CEO is the named owner of AI outcomes, against where accountability sits diffusely across the C-suite:

This is cross-sectional and self-reported. Reverse causality is not excluded: organisations already succeeding may simply be more willing to name the CEO as owner. No published study directly tests single-owner against federated as designed alternatives. Treat the direction as well supported and the magnitude as indicative.

Two structural findings sit alongside it. Accenture's September 2026 work found 42 percent of organisations have no single owner of AI cost and outcomes, relying on shared IT and finance accountability. BCG's 2025 survey of 1,250 executives found the highest performers are not centralised or federated but both: 43 percent have joint business and IT ownership, they are three times more likely to have a chief AI officer, and C-suite engagement runs near 100 percent against 8 percent for laggards.

Denominate in outcomes, not tokens

This is the most actionable disagreement in the whole evidence base, and almost every enterprise dashboard is currently on the wrong side of it.

Uber published the clearest working model in August 2026. They decompose AI cost multiplicatively, giving each term a named optimisation owner:

Uber's cost decomposition

Users × Sessions per user × Turns per session × Requests per turn × Tokens per request × Price per token

Their production metrics are denominated in outcomes, not tokens: cost per merged pull request, cost per review, cost per alert, cost per cleanup. Result over February to August 2026: cost per thousand model requests down 34 percent from peak, cost per session down 52 percent from its June peak, while weekly active employees grew 7x and weekly agent requests grew 9.4x, with total spend broadly flat.

DoiT's August 2026 research gives the arithmetic for why token denomination misprices reliability:

  • Cost per solved task = expected cost per attempt ÷ success probability
  • Risk-adjusted CPT = CPT + (leak rate × cleanup cost)

On their measured workloads the ranking between two frontier models flips depending on whether failure cleanup is priced, and again depending on whether the workload is coding or tool use. Run-to-run variance on identical agentic tasks reached 30x. Tokenizer differences alone shift token counts by around 30 percent for the same text. Any chargeback model denominated in tokens will systematically reward the model that fails cheaply over the model that succeeds.

The levers that actually move the number

Before renegotiating anything, three engineering levers have published, verifiable economics:

Cache time-to-live is a real decision with a published rationale. Uber runs a one-hour TTL for interactive developer sessions because engineers pause for more than five minutes while thinking, and a five-minute TTL for subagents because they run single tasks. The break-even is governed by the expected idle gap, not by prompt size.

Where the evidence disagrees

Is 95 percent of enterprise AI failing, or is 80 percent of it working?

The pessimistic case

MIT NANDA (2025): 95 percent of organisations getting zero return on 30 to 40 billion dollars. S&P Global (March 2025): 42 percent scrapped most AI initiatives, up from 17 percent a year earlier; 46 percent of proofs of concept abandoned. McKinsey: EBIT attribution flat across two waves, 6 percent high performers. KPMG: 7 percent with established ROI. Gartner (June 2025): over 40 percent of agentic AI projects cancelled by end 2027, on "escalating costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls."

The optimistic case

Bain (November 2025, n=197): 80 percent of generative AI use cases met or exceeded expectations, 78 percent of those with measurable revenue or cost movement. IDC and Microsoft (2024): 3.70 dollars returned per dollar spent, top performers 10.30. Omdia and Snowflake (2025, n=2,050): 92 percent of early adopters report positive return. EY (May 2026): 98 percent report positive ROI.

What actually explains the gap. MIT's 95 percent counts all surveyed organisations including the roughly 80 percent that never ran a custom pilot; on its own data, around 25 percent of custom pilots succeeded, which is inside Bain's 20 to 40 percent range by function. MIT required sustained P&L impact within six months; IDC measured value realisation at thirteen. Omdia is explicit that only 40 percent of its respondents actually quantified the return behind the 92 percent. EY's own data undercuts its 98 percent: 82 percent are worried about token cost and only 3 percent hit their planned budget allocation. IDC was commissioned by Microsoft, Omdia by Snowflake, and MIT NANDA's authors recommended their own project as the remedy.

Working position. Roughly 35 to 40 percent of enterprises report some EBIT contribution. Five to eight percent report material contribution. Twenty to forty percent of pilots by function reach production. Under ten percent have a quantified, auditable return. Every number outside that band has an identifiable methodological or commercial reason for being outside it.

Data readiness and integration

Data readiness is a gate on one use case, not a programme that precedes all of them. Treating it as a programme is its own failure mode.

On the reference architecture
Data InIngest & PrepareData Acquisition & IntegrationData Governance & TrustData & Knowledge Layer

The left half of the picture: where data comes in, how it is ingested and prepared, and the two layers that make it trustworthy and meaningful. The eight-test gate below decides which of these parts a single use case actually needs, so the foundation is funded by use cases rather than ahead of them.

This is the consideration most often quoted and least often understood. The famous statistic is real. The reading most people take from it is wrong.

What Gartner actually said

The exact wording: "Through 2026, organizations will abandon 60% of AI projects unsupported by AI-ready data" (Gartner, February 2025). Note the scope. It is not 60 percent of AI projects. It is 60 percent of AI projects unsupported by AI-ready data. Secondary coverage drops that clause almost universally.

Gartner's definition of AI-ready is also not what most people assume: "Data must be representative of the use case, of every pattern, errors, outliers and unexpected emergence that is needed to train or run an AI model for a specific use." Gartner is explicit that high data quality is not the same as AI-ready, and that representative data "may include poor-quality data, too." Melody Chien of Gartner puts the scoping plainly: "AI-ready data means that data is ready to support certain AI cases." The same data can be ready for one use case and not another.

Three independent studies converge on the same narrow band: 7 percent of enterprises say their data is completely ready for AI (Cloudera and Harvard Business Review Analytic Services, March 2026), 6 percent say fully ready to support AI at scale (Dun & Bradstreet, July 2026, n=10,000 businesses across 32 countries), 7 percent qualify as Accenture's data reinventors. The reinventor group carries a 4.5 percentage point EBIT margin advantage over peers.

The self-assessment problem

The Precisely and Drexel LeBow study (n=505 data and analytics leaders, published January 2026) found 88 percent believe their data is AI-ready while 43 percent simultaneously cite data readiness as a barrier, in the same survey. The same pattern repeats on infrastructure (87 percent claim it, 42 percent cite it as a challenge) and skills (86 percent claim sufficiency, 41 percent identify a gap).

This is why the 7 percent figures and the 88 percent figures are both true. The dispersion is mostly an artifact of what "ready" was allowed to mean. Anyone quoting "only 7 percent" owes you the definition.

The strongest causal evidence in this entire document

One comparison to avoid making. The Spider 2.0 public leaderboard now shows scores in the nineties on the Snow subset. Those are heavily scaffolded agent systems on a curated subset, not raw model accuracy on the full set. Putting 96.7 percent and 10.1 percent on the same axis would be misleading.

Unstructured data is where the gap is widest

  • Around 20 percent of unstructured data is AI-ready on average, and only 7 percent of organisations have more than half of theirs prepared (Snowflake, March 2026).
  • 70 percent of data and AI leaders report that less than half their unstructured data is discoverable and usable for AI (BARC, June 2026, n=225).
  • Chunking benchmarks show the ceiling moves with document structure rather than pipeline sophistication. Recursive splitting at 512 tokens reached 69 percent end-to-end accuracy; semantic chunking reached 54 percent because its fragments averaged 43 tokens, retrieving cleanly but giving the model too little to answer with.
The metric vendors quote, and the metric that matters

Chroma Research reports 91.9 percent token-level retrieval recall for semantic chunking on general text. FloTorch reports 54 percent end-to-end accuracy for the same strategy. Both are true and they are not the same measurement. High retrieval recall does not imply correct answers. Insist on end-to-end numbers in every vendor conversation.

Two governance facts that change how you index

Embeddings are personal data. Research on embedding inversion reconstructs 92 percent of 32-token text inputs exactly from their vectors. Independent replication recovered 40 percent of personally identifiable information exactly from sentence-length text. Redaction before embedding drove recovery to zero. A vector index inherits the classification of its source text and must carry the same access control and residency constraints.

Deleting from a vector store usually does not delete. The June 2026 "Ghost Vectors" research found that vector databases typically implement deletion as a metadata soft delete, leaving the embedding physically on disk. Measured recovery from soft-deleted vectors: 25.5 percent of person names and 46.4 percent of geographic locations from Wikipedia biographical data, 49.9 percent patient surname recovery from synthetic clinical data, 99.17 percent identity recovery from facial embeddings. A deletion request satisfied at the API layer is very likely not satisfied at the storage layer. If you have committed to an erasure right, you need cryptographic overwrite or media destruction, not a delete call.

Permissions are the third one, and it is the one that has already embarrassed people publicly. Retrieval assistants honour existing permissions exactly. They do not create a leak; they make a latent one legible at conversational speed. Remediate sharing before indexing, not by filtering after retrieval.

Where the evidence disagrees

Is data readiness the real blocker, or is it consultant product that delays everything?

Readiness is binding

BEAVER's 0 percent on private warehouses. The semantic-layer lift exceeding the model-upgrade lift across three independent settings. Accenture's 72 percent lacking trusted data and 80 percent altering initiatives because of data risk. McKinsey (April 2026): eight in ten companies cite data limitations as a roadblock to scaling agents, and fewer than 10 percent have scaled them.

Readiness is overstated

dbt Labs' own benchmark shows raw text-to-SQL on an unmodelled schema rising from 32.7 percent in 2023 to 64.5 percent in 2026 with no data work at all. Models are absorbing some of the burden, measurably. McKinsey's own August 2026 survey does not name data as the top barrier, attributing constraint to organisational absorption capacity instead. And the argument from practice: perfect data does not exist anywhere, including inside the most data-sophisticated companies in the world.

Honest assessment of the counter-case: weak on evidence, right on framing. Most of it is anecdote or vendor content. The one rigorously measured piece, the dbt doubling, comes from a study whose own conclusion is pro-semantic-layer, and in the same table the semantic layer still wins by eight to sixteen points and reaches near 100 percent where raw generation reaches 84 to 90. But the counter-case is correct about one thing that the readiness camp consistently gets wrong.

Working position. Readiness is per use case, which is Gartner's own definition. "Get the data ready" as an enterprise-wide programme before any use case ships is a failure mode both camps should reject. Gate each use case on the eight tests below, ship it, and let the gate failures tell you which parts of the foundation to fund. That sequence gives you a funded roadmap instead of a two-year prerequisite.

The gate: eight tests before a use case is allowed to start

A named user, task, output and numeric quality threshold

Not "improve customer service." A person, a task they do, the artefact produced, and the accuracy below which it is worthless.

Data representative of the use case, including its errors and outliers

Gartner's definition, not a cleanliness score. Representative beats clean.

Metrics defined in a machine-readable semantic layer

If the definition of active customer lives in a dashboard or an analyst's head, the agent will invent its own.

Certified example queries and join-path metadata for the relevant tables

This is where LinkedIn's 9 to 53 percent came from. It is cheap and it is the highest-yield work available.

Source permissions remediated before indexing

Not filtered after retrieval. Broad grants get fixed or the content does not get indexed.

Vector indexes carry the source classification and residency, with a real deletion path

Overwrite, not soft delete. Confirm with your vector store vendor in writing.

An oversight tier assigned

Automated, sampled, reviewed or advisory. See consideration 06.

A named cost owner

42 percent of organisations do not have one. That is the cheapest gap on this list to close.

Infrastructure and architecture

Start with the simplest shape that could work. Complexity should be something the system earned, not something it started with.

On the reference architecture
Reasoning & MemoryModel Compute & OrchestrationAI Model CoreControl plane

The inner rings and the control plane: context retrieval and memory, the model gateway, tool use and agent runtime, and the frontier and domain models at the core. The workflow-versus-agent choice below is made in workflow orchestration; the context engineering techniques live in reasoning and memory.

The published engineering position from the people building these systems is consistently anti-complexity. That is worth stating loudly, because the market incentive runs the other way.

Anthropic's own guidance: "When building applications with LLMs, we recommend finding the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed" and "For many applications, optimizing single LLM calls with retrieval and in-context examples is usually enough." The distinction that matters is not agent versus no agent. It is workflow versus agent:

ShapeDefinitionUse when
WorkflowLLMs and tools orchestrated through predefined code paths: prompt chaining, routing, parallelisation, orchestrator-workers, evaluator-optimiserThe steps are predictable. This covers most enterprise work, and it is cheaper, testable and debuggable.
AgentThe model dynamically directs its own process and tool use, controlling how it accomplishes the taskOpen-ended problems where you cannot predict the number of steps or hardcode the path, and the task value justifies the cost.

Gartner's agent-washing finding belongs here rather than in a governance section, because it is an architecture problem: "Many use cases positioned as agentic today don't require agentic implementations." Menlo Ventures found only 16 percent of enterprise deployments qualify as true agents; the rest are fixed-sequence or routing-based workflows. That is not a criticism. It is the correct architecture being mislabelled by its vendors.

Context engineering is now the discipline that decides quality

Large context windows did not remove the constraint. They moved it. Models have an attention budget, and "every new token introduced depletes this budget by some amount," because transformer attention creates n-squared pairwise relationships across n tokens.

Chroma's context rot research, across 18 models, found performance degrades significantly as input length grows even on simple tasks. Low semantic similarity between question and target degrades faster than high similarity. Distractors compound non-uniformly. All models performed significantly better on focused prompts than on full ones.

The operational answer, four techniques
  • Compaction. Summarise a conversation approaching the limit and reinitialise with the summary. Uber runs automatic compaction at 400,000 tokens even on one-million-token models, which tells you the limit that binds is attention, not capacity.
  • Memory outside the window. Agents write notes to durable storage and pull them back on demand, giving persistent memory with minimal context overhead.
  • Sub-agent isolation. Specialised sub-agents work in clean context windows and return a distilled summary, typically 1,000 to 2,000 tokens, rather than their whole trajectory.
  • Just-in-time retrieval. Hold lightweight identifiers such as file paths and stored queries, and load content at runtime. Hybrid beats either extreme: seed context up front for speed, allow exploration for the rest.

The single largest waste in most enterprise agent deployments is tool definitions. Loading a full set of connector tool definitions up front against progressive disclosure through code execution: 150,000 tokens down to 2,000, a 98.7 percent reduction. Uber independently measured the same thing on their MCP estate, cutting 50,000 to 70,000 tokens per request to near zero, with batched tool calls in code reducing token usage 55 to 100 percent.

Reliability arithmetic, and why pass@1 is a marketing metric

The tau-bench work introduced pass^k to measure whether an agent succeeds repeatedly. The finding: state-of-the-art function-calling agents succeed on under 50 percent of tasks on a single attempt, and pass^8 falls below 25 percent in the retail domain. For production, pass^k is the honest metric. Single-attempt accuracy tells you what the demo will look like.

The compounding is simple arithmetic and it is worth doing out loud with any vendor. At 98 percent per-step success, a 50-step task succeeds about 36 percent of the time. At 99 percent, about 61 percent. This is why the production response to unreliability is not a better model. It is checkpointing and verification that reset the error budget: commits per increment, durable execution that resumes at the failed step rather than restarting, circuit breakers on step count and cost.

On capability trajectory, use the primary source and its confidence intervals rather than the headline. METR's revised time-horizon work (January 2026) puts the doubling time of task length that frontier agents complete at 50 percent reliability at 196.5 days across the full period, 130.8 days since 2023, and 88.6 days since 2024. Their reported 50 percent horizons carry wide intervals: Claude Opus 4.5 at 320 minutes with a 50 percent interval of 170 to 729. That is a 4.3x spread. Plan against the bottom of the interval.

Buy the application, centralise the access. That is what the market has converged on and what the published platform teams actually own. Uber runs an AI Gateway and a separate MCP Gateway fronting more than 1,000 MCP servers behind unified authentication and policy. Netflix built in-house serving on vLLM behind an OpenAI-compatible interface specifically to get deep customisation of constrained decoding and output validation inside their existing production environment.

The argument for the gateway is not elegance. It is that the cost equation, the model-churn migration, the authentication and policy boundary, and the evaluation gate are all cross-cutting concerns. Uber holding total spend broadly flat through 9.4x request growth is an argument for the gateway, not for any individual optimisation. On the reference architecture, that centralised access is the model gateway, and the cross-cutting concerns are the control plane along the bottom.

Where the evidence disagrees

Should you build multi-agent systems at all? Two well-argued positions, published one day apart in June 2025, and the reconciliation that arrived ten months later.

Against

Cognition: "Don't build multi-agents." Decision-making becomes too dispersed and context cannot be shared thoroughly enough between agents. Actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results. Recommendation: single-threaded linear agents with context compression for long tasks.

For

Anthropic: a multi-agent research system outperformed the single-agent equivalent by 90.2 percent on their research evaluation. But the same post scopes it tightly: good for breadth-first parallelisable search, bad for anything needing shared context, heavy interdependencies, real-time coordination, or most coding tasks. And it costs roughly 15x the tokens of chat.

The reconciling variable is whether subagents write. Cognition's own April 2026 follow-up states it: "writes stay single-threaded and the additional agents contribute intelligence rather than actions." Their working exceptions are a review agent with no prior context catching around two bugs per pull request, 58 percent of them severe; a weaker primary calling a stronger model on hard cases; and hierarchical delegation, which needed more context engineering than expected.

Working position. Read-only subagents returning distilled summaries: multi-agent wins. Subagents writing to shared artefacts with no external arbiter: single-threaded wins. Subagents writing with deterministic locking and a near-perfect test oracle: multi-agent can work, and has, but that is a condition, not an exception. Below a task value that justifies 15x tokens: use a workflow. Note also that "multi-agent" in survey literature usually means "more than one prompt," while in this debate it means "more than one locus of autonomous decision-making." Do not let the word do the work.

The supply chain nobody inventories

In September 2025 a malicious MCP server was published to npm. Regulatory position as of September 2026

If your compliance plan says August 2026, it is out of date

The EU AI Omnibus was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 and entered into force on 27 July 2026. Stand-alone Annex III high-risk obligations moved from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027. Embedded high-risk under Annex I moved from 2 August 2027 to 2 August 2028.

What did not move. Article 50 transparency obligations still applied from 2 August 2026. The deferral covers Chapter III only. Anyone who deferred their whole programme still has live transparency exposure today, and anyone who built a 2026 conformity programme now has sixteen months of slack to use properly.

Article 14 is the human oversight clause and its wording is worth having exactly: high-risk systems must be designed "in such a way, including with appropriate human-machine interface tools, that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons during the period in which they are in use." Three details most summaries omit:

  • Article 14(3) makes oversight proportionate to the system's risks, autonomy level and use context. This is the legal basis for tiering rather than uniform review.
  • Article 14(4) names automation bias explicitly. The overseer must be able to recognise it. That converts "the human approved it" from a defence into a potential compliance failure.
  • Article 14(5) is the only numeric oversight mandate in the Act: for remote biometric identification, separate verification by at least two competent natural persons. That is the strongest legal precedent for four-eyes review as a control rather than a nicety.

Article 12 requires automatic event logging over the lifetime of the system, with a six-month minimum retention. It specifies no format, no required fields and no tamper-proofing requirement. Logs that can be edited without detection have no evidentiary value. The regulation creates a logging duty without creating an integrity duty, so append-only or cryptographically chained logging is your decision to make, not the regulator's.

Two things that shifted the liability picture

Model risk management has a hole in it. On 17 April 2026 the US banking agencies issued revised model risk management guidance superseding the 2011 originals. The revised guidance explicitly excludes generative and agentic AI from its scope on the grounds that these technologies are novel and rapidly evolving. Annual validation mandates were dropped, vendor contingency plan requirements eliminated, and a materiality concept introduced. The regulators looked at LLMs, concluded the 2011 validation paradigm does not fit, and removed them from scope rather than adapting it. The classic validation triad assumes a stable artefact you can re-run; a hosted model is non-deterministic, silently re-versioned and opaque as to training data. That leaves a governance vacuum exactly where the novel risk sits, and filling it is now the institution's problem rather than the supervisor's.

The insurance backstop is being withdrawn. In January 2026 a generative AI exclusion was introduced for commercial general liability policies, excluding coverage for bodily injury, property damage and personal or advertising injury arising out of generative AI. Broad AI exclusions are appearing in directors and officers, employment practices and fiduciary policies with language expansive enough to capture ordinary business activities involving AI. This is the strongest non-regulatory argument for governance available: if AI failure has moved from an insured loss to an uninsured balance-sheet loss, governance is no longer a compliance cost. It is the substitute for a risk transfer mechanism that has been taken away.

And on whether the vendor is responsible rather than you: in Moffatt v. Air Canada (February 2024) the tribunal rejected the argument that the chatbot was a separate entity accountable for its own actions. "While a chatbot has an interactive component, it is still just a part of Air Canada's website." And: "It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website." No novel AI liability theory was required. Existing misrepresentation law reached the outcome.

Organisational readiness

The bottleneck does not disappear when a task is automated. It moves one level up, to whoever reviews the output and carries the accountability.

On the reference architecture
Deliver & ActIntelligent ConsumptionHuman Decision Makers

The right-hand side: where answers, insights and actions are delivered, and the people who receive them. The review capacity this section asks you to fund is the human decision makers box, staffed and given the time.

This is where most published advice is weakest, because the people who publish on it sell change programmes. So here is the evidence, including the case against the advice.

On the widely quoted 10-20-70 rule, that 70 percent of effort should go to people and process: the wording is confirmed on BCG's own site, but no publication gives an originating study, dataset or derivation. Treat it as a useful heuristic and a rhetorical anchor. Do not present it as a measured finding, because it is not one.

What is actually measured

The closest quantified analogue comes from Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index (n=20,000 knowledge workers, 10 countries): organisational factors such as culture, manager support and talent practices drive 67 percent of reported AI impact, against 32 percent for individual mindset and behaviour. Managers who model AI use produce a 17-point lift in perceived value; managers who set quality standards produce a 22-point increase in critical thinking. Note the vendor interest: this is a conclusion Microsoft benefits from.

McKinsey's 2026 survey is cleaner on the mechanism: 73 percent of high performers are redesigning workflows against 25 percent of everyone else, and high performers are 3.3x more likely to expect business transformation within three years. Deloitte's parallel finding is that only 30 percent are redesigning key processes and 37 percent use AI with minimal process change at all.

The productivity evidence is genuinely contradictory, and the resolution is precise

The jagged frontier finding resolves the contradiction. Gains concentrate in less experienced people on tasks inside the model's competence. Penalties concentrate in experts working outside it, through over-reliance on plausible but wrong output. The levelling effect is consistent: below-average performers gained 43 percent against 17 percent for above-average, and the largest coding gains went to the least experienced. Targeting enablement at your high performers inverts the evidence.

Anyone still citing "19 percent slower" as a settled fact is citing a superseded result. METR redesigned the experiment in February 2026 because participants were withholding tasks they would not attempt without AI. The revised figures are minus 18 percent among original participants with a confidence interval of minus 38 to plus 9, and minus 4 percent among new recruits with an interval of minus 15 to plus 9. Both intervals cross zero, and METR states the true speedup is likely higher than the figures suggest.

The finding that survives all of this is DORA's, and it is the one to design around: AI adoption now shows a positive relationship with delivery throughput and a continuing negative relationship with delivery stability. More output, more failures. Thirty percent of respondents report little or no trust in AI-generated code. AI is an amplifier of whatever the team already is.

The bottleneck moves up, and it is unfunded

Harvard Business Review research (June 2026, 18 interviews across two consulting firms) found a consistent pattern: senior leaders used AI strategically for wider scope and smaller teams, junior staff achieved dramatic productivity gains, and middle managers absorbed three new unfunded duties: validating AI-generated output, identifying errors, and coaching AI competence, with unchanged or increased delivery pressure and no formal support.

Put that next to DORA's stability finding and the conclusion is uncomfortable. More throughput with worse stability means the review, test and rollback functions need more capacity, not less, precisely when headcount logic says cut them. The sample is small and consulting-specific, but it is the only direct evidence on this question and it points the same way as the delivery metrics.

Where the evidence disagrees

Is change management essential, or is it consultant product for a problem good tools solve by themselves?

It is largely product

Shadow adoption is enormous and entirely unmanaged: around 90 percent personal AI use for work, 66 percent regular use, a consumer assistant topping expense reports across 40 million licences. What does not happen by itself is workflow redesign, review capacity, accountability reassignment, stability protection and measurement. So the honest reframing is not 70 percent people and process. It is close to zero percent communications, a great deal of process and platform, and a specific, currently underfunded amount of managerial review capacity. Fund the review layer first; it is the only part of this that is both measured and ignored.

Governance and risk management

The failure being corrected across 2026 is not oversight. It is uniform oversight, which spends the review budget on low-consequence work and produces rubber-stamping where it matters.

On the reference architecture
Judgment & Trust GateDecision TracesGovernance & GuardrailsObservability & Evaluation

The judgment and trust gate at the top of the picture is the oversight tiers below, made concrete. The decision traces in reasoning and memory, and the observability and evaluation service in the control plane, are the evidence those tiers depend on.

Tier on three variables: how far the consequence reaches, whether it can be reversed, and whether the action was authenticated.

That triple comes from the Cloud Security Alliance's agentic profile work (April 2026). It is the most usable tier-assignment rule published, and it is compatible with the only proportionality language in the EU AI Act, Article 14(3), which ties oversight intensity to risk, autonomy level and use context.

The synthesis is mine.

Evaluation is the control, and most evaluation is misreported

If you have delegated quality measurement to a model judging another model, this is the most important research published in 2026 on the subject. A systematic evaluation of 21 judge models across 9 providers (June 2026) found:

  • Raw agreement overstates discriminative ability by 33.8 to 41.2 percentage points, cohort mean 38.6. If you are reporting your judge's raw agreement with human labels as your quality evidence, you are overstating it by roughly 39 points. Report chance-corrected agreement.
  • Position bias ranges from 0.002 to 0.192 across judges, roughly a hundredfold spread, and two production-deployed models exceeded 0.10.
  • The consistency-bias paradox. Models achieved test-retest reliability above 0.95 while simultaneously showing position bias above 0.10. The authors call it reproducibility without validity. A judge that is stable is not thereby a judge that is right.
  • Verbosity bias, long assumed at 20 to 40 percent variance, measured under 0.011 across all 21 models under a single pairwise rubric. If your evaluation policy controls for it, that control may be solving a problem that does not exist.
  • Models shifted up to 15 ranking positions across benchmarks. Your benchmark choice is a bigger lever than your model choice.

There is a second measurement problem underneath: reference instability. Airbnb's evaluation engineering work found around 75 percent of generated references differ across labelling runs on identical inputs, while the true signal they were trying to detect was 1 to 3 percent. Most observed variance is noise. If your evaluation sample is sized for convenience, you are measuring your own jitter.

And golden sets decay as control artefacts even when the model has not changed. Evaluation datasets need versioning and expiry the same way models do. There is currently no NIST, ISO or regulatory-published evaluation methodology for production LLM systems. NIST's generative AI profile tells you what to measure conceptually across its twelve risk categories; it does not tell you how to gate a deployment. That gap is yours to fill and to document.

Audit evidence: what you will be asked for

Article 14(4) requires the overseer to correctly interpret the output. Colorado's replacement law requires a plain-language explanation of a decision and the role of the automated system within 30 days of an adverse outcome. Both presume the decision can be explained afterwards. For a non-deterministic model on a vendor endpoint that may have been silently re-versioned, you cannot re-run the decision.

The only defensible substitute is capturing, at decision time: the full input context, the retrieved documents, every tool call and its result, the exact model identifier, and the sampling parameters. That is a design decision made before the incident, not after. ISO/IEC 42001 is what auditors are increasingly asking for as evidence of a governed management system, and it is certifiable, which matters when the question is whether you can show a third party rather than assert it. On the reference architecture these are the decision traces, and they are what the evidence and provenance control draws on.

On agents specifically, the standards work is real and immature. Decentralised identifiers and workload identity credentials issued through an agent name service, agents registered in existing identity infrastructure, a delegation register tracking authority lineage, and pre-authorised automatic suspension for high-severity incidents. Several IETF drafts exist on agent authentication and trust negotiation; all are individual submissions with no consensus status, so do not cite them as standards. Agent spend limits are a genuine white space: no published framework treats them as a first-class control, and they are trivially easy to implement and easy to forget.

Scale and evolution

The model underneath you will change every few weeks. Build the capability to absorb that, not a project that assumes it will not.

On the reference architecture
Model RegistryEnvironment & DeploymentModel GatewayContinuous Learning & Feedback

The control plane along the bottom: the registry that pins model versions, the environment and deployment service that shadows and promotes them, the gateway that routes between them, and the feedback loop that keeps the whole picture current as the models at the core change.

This is the pivot from managed to resilient, and it is the one that separates a platform from a collection of successful pilots.

Model churn is an operating condition, not an incident

Uber states the reality plainly: models are continuously re-evaluated because the frontier shifts every few weeks. Every managed agent carries its own real-work benchmark precisely so re-evaluation is mechanical rather than a judgment call each time. The practices that hold up:

  • Pin explicit dated model identifiers, never floating aliases. This is near-universal practice and almost never written down.
  • Shadow the new model against production traffic and gate on a threshold. Ramp runs its policy agent in shadow on real transactions and activates only when shadow accuracy clears a defined bar, with curated golden datasets reviewed internally rather than derived from user approvals, which reflect convenience rather than correctness.
  • Rainbow, not blue-green. An agent mid-trajectory cannot be swapped, because its state lives in an in-flight context window. This is a genuinely distinctive infrastructure requirement of agentic systems.
  • Hold the model constant when measuring cost work, or the two variables confound. Uber's 34 percent reduction is credible precisely because they said the model was held constant.
The migration-day cost event nobody budgets for

A model version change invalidates the entire prompt cache prefix. Every request that was reading cache at 0.1x becomes a cache write at 1.25x or 2x, simultaneously, across all traffic. Uber's session-analysis dashboard flags cache expiration inefficiency as one of sixteen tracked anti-patterns, which tells you it is a real operational cost. I could not find a published post-mortem quantifying the spike, so treat the magnitude as unquantified and the direction as certain. Model your migration day, do not discover it.
