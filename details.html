How we might work together

Three ways we can work together.

Each is a fixed piece of work with a clear finish, and leaves your team with something it owns and understands. None of them is an open-ended transformation programme.

01 · Foundations4 to 8 weeks

Get your data ready for AI

When this helpsSeveral AI pilots are giving your leadership different answers to the same question.

Together we write down the business meaning behind the 30 to 50 numbers your organisation actually runs on: what each one means, where it comes from, who looks after it, and the traps people have learned to step around. We follow industry standards where they help, such as BIAN for banking or FHIR for healthcare. Then we test it honestly: how accurately your AI answers your real questions, before and after, not a generic benchmark.

What stays with your teamAn agreed, documented set of business definitions your AI can use, a scorecard of how ready your data is, and a 90-day plan for what to fix next.
02 · Reliability3 to 6 weeks

Make your AI agent reliable enough to launch

When this helpsYour AI agent works in the demo but fails with real users.

I sit with your own experts to turn their knowledge into a test set, run each case repeatedly, and share plainly how often the agent is right and how it fails. Then we review the design around it together: whether it should be a fixed workflow or an agent, what it is allowed to read, how documents are prepared, what records it keeps, and what it costs to run.

What stays with your teamA reliability number the board can trust, and a design your team feels ready to build.

Together we design where you are heading, from the source systems through shared business definitions to reports, AI agents and automated actions. We agree how people and AI agents will work alongside each other, and plan the steps: pilot to daily use, one project to many, fragile to dependable.

Chapter IWhere AI projects get stuck
Chapter I · What I'm brought in to do

Where AI projects get stuck.

AI projects rarely fail because of the technology. They stall when nobody owns the next step. These are the three stages I'm brought in for. They come roughly in order, though most organisations are working on two at once, and most are at the second one right now.

Stage 1
Pilot→Daily use

Get it working for real customers

The demo worked. Real use means deciding what happens when the AI is wrong in front of a customer, who signs that off, and what the backup plan is. Those are business decisions, not engineering tasks, which is why they get put off.

The warning sign: an 18-month-old pilot everyone still calls promising.
Stage 2
One project→Many

Make the next one faster and cheaper

One AI system in daily use is an achievement. Twenty is a capability. That needs shared foundations, well-managed data, and a way of delivering that other teams can repeat, rather than one star team starting from scratch each time.

The warning sign: every new AI project costs as much as the first one did.
Stage 3
Fragile→Dependable

Make it run well without me

Three things at once: it keeps working when the AI model or vendor underneath it changes, you can show a regulator evidence of how it decides rather than promises, and your own team runs it confidently after I leave.

The warning sign: nobody can explain why the system made last Tuesday's decision.

How I work

Judgment first, technology second.

"Anyone can wire up an intelligent system now. The hard part is sensibility: knowing which problem deserves the intelligence, what the output means in context, and when to overrule a confident machine. That judgment is the architecture. The diagram is just its residue."

After twenty-five years in regulated industries, the pattern holds. The platforms that lasted were the ones where someone made the calls a model can't: asking the right question before building the answer.

  1. 01

    Agree the right problem first

    Before any AI is built, agree which problem is worth solving. The costliest mistake is a clever answer to the wrong question.

  2. 02

    Make sure the system knows what the data means

    Shared business definitions, clear data ownership, and a record of where every number comes from, so the AI's answers can be trusted.

  3. 03

    Then choose and build the technology

    Real-time data streams, data platforms, AI search, AI agents. The technology comes once the questions and definitions are settled.

  4. 04

    Keep a person able to overrule it

    Design the system so a person can step in. AI working at machine speed still needs someone who can say "stop."

Chapter II · Selected stories

Nine projects I helped get unstuck.

Regulatory · Major BankStage 3 · Fragile to dependable

No one owned the data, so the regulator's questions went unanswered.

What was happening
Regulators asked questions the data couldn't answer. The cause: twelve teams, twelve versions of the truth, no shared ownership.
How we worked through it
Gave each of the twelve teams ownership of its own data, on a shared Data Vault 2.0 model, with a small central team guiding rather than policing. Trust first, platform second.
✓Regulators could see the bank's risk clearly again; teams aligned
National Tax AuthorityStage 1 · Pilot to daily use

Investigators didn't trust the fraud scores, so they ignored them.

What was happening
A capable fraud-detection system sat unused. Nobody had explained how it scored cases, so investigators worked around it.
How we worked through it
Redesigned the investigator workflow alongside the model. Co-designed the interface and built transparency into every score with the people using it.
10%yearly growth in the tax base · cases closed 50% faster
Real-Time PaymentsStage 3 · Fragile to dependable

Staff kept overriding a fraud-alert system they didn't understand.

What was happening
A real-time fraud system (built on Kafka) worked well technically, but the operations team routinely overrode it because the alerts made no sense to them.
How we worked through it
Rebuilt the alert logic with the fraud team: thresholds, explanations, exception flows co-designed, then re-engineered the platform around those decisions.
↓Far fewer overrides; more fraud caught
Generative AI · LendingStage 1 · Pilot to daily use

Analysts refused an AI tool that made the decisions for them.

What was happening
An AI system for customer identity checks (KYC) was rejected by the lending team. Almost nobody used it: the AI decided, the analysts just watched.
How we worked through it
Turned it into an assistant. Rebuilt it so analysts made the decisions and the AI brought them the evidence. Workshops with the analysts came before any code.
↑Widely used; checks done faster
Predictive AnalyticsStage 1 · Pilot to daily use

Managers ignored a sound prediction of which customers would leave.

What was happening
The model gave each customer a score between 0 and 1. Managers ignored it: a number told them nothing about what to do.
How we worked through it
Replaced scores with plain-language narratives and ranked actions. Rebuilt around the only question that mattered: what do I do next?
→From ignored to cited in every review
ORIAN.Data PlatformStage 2 · One project to many

AI gave confident answers nobody could explain or check.

What was happening
AI investments kept producing confident answers nobody could stand behind. Teams couldn't explain decisions, executives couldn't verify them.
How we worked through it
Designed the ORIAN.Data platform so every answer comes with its context, from shared business definitions, and an explanation of how it was reached.
★In use with clients in regulated industries
Global data platform · AzureStage 2 · One project to many

A data platform stalled because every division defined the same numbers differently.

What was happening
A multi-million data platform (a lakehouse) stalled after six months. The problem was agreement between divisions, not technology.
How we worked through it
Ran business-alignment workshops before architecture. Built a shared glossary every division signed off, then designed the platform around that language.
1One source of truth, agreed before built
Data Mesh · Enterprise BankStage 2 · One project to many

Every data request took six to eight weeks.

What was happening
One central team of thirty served twelve business areas. Requests queued for six to eight weeks. The business felt powerless; the team felt unappreciated.
How we worked through it
Changed how people worked first: each business area designed its own data in workshops, and the central team became helpers instead of gatekeepers. The technology (a data mesh) followed.
↑Teams serve themselves; answers come faster
50TB Migration · UtilityStage 3 · Fragile to dependable

A 50TB migration team afraid of being blamed if it went wrong.

What was happening
11,748 tables, 49.65 TB, 1,550 data pipelines. The plan was solid, but the engineers were anxious because they couldn't see the whole picture.
How we worked through it
Made the whole plan visible, step by step and risk by risk. Every engineer could see what moved, when, and how to undo it. Visibility replaced fear.
50TBPhased delivery, on plan, confident team
